"Made in Tennessee" describes where the vehicle was assembled.

It does not describe where the materials inside it came from.

The VW ID.4 assembled in Chattanooga is the most important version of the T1 Blindspot story — not the German motor case from Blog 1 in this series. Because this one removes every geographic intuition a compliance team might use to argue the exposure away.

The vehicle is domestic. The battery cells are assembled in Georgia. Both facts are true and both are irrelevant to the tariff question.

Section 301 and IEEPA fire at the US border when the imported input crosses. They apply to the battery cell imported from Korea — not to the vehicle assembled from it in Tennessee. The liability attaches to the material content of the imported component. The Tennessee assembly line does not dissolve it.

Inside that cell: approximately 60 kg of graphite per vehicle. China processes more than 90% of global battery-grade graphite. The tariff rate on Chinese-origin graphite is **93.5%** — anti-dumping duties, Section 301, and IEEPA stacked. The highest rate of any EV material in the current regime.

At 60 kg × $5/kg × 93.5%: **$280.50 per vehicle** in conservative tariff exposure from one material, sitting undocumented inside a Georgia-assembled battery cell inside a Tennessee-assembled vehicle.

Add the NdFeB from the motor stator ($213/vehicle) and the silicon steel laminations ($37.50/vehicle): **$531 per vehicle. $79.65M per year** across one programme at 150,000 units.

Three materials. Three tariff regimes. One vehicle assembled in America.

All of it undocumented until the material graph traces it.

The most important word in trade compliance right now is not "China." It is "origin."
