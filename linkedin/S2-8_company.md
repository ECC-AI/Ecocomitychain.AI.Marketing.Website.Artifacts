BMW was notified in January 2024 that a banned component was in their supply chain.

They continued importing for three more months.

The Senate Finance Committee named this in their May 2024 report. The component was from Sichuan Jingweida Technology Group — added to the UFLPA Entity List in December 2023. The notification came from Lear Corporation, a Tier 2 supplier, in January 2024. BMW did not stop importing affected vehicles until April 2024.

The committee's conclusion: automakers' self-policing is clearly not doing the job.

That conclusion is accurate. But it misidentifies the cause.

BMW did not ignore the notification. BMW had no system capable of answering the question the notification created: of the thousands of components across dozens of vehicle lines, which specific ones contain JWD material? In which assemblies? Across which shipments? Which ones do we stop?

A supplier-level flag creates a search problem. When the flagged entity is at T3 or T4 — as JWD was — the search space spans thousands of components across multiple production programmes. Standard compliance platforms cannot navigate it.

The notification told them a banned entity was somewhere in their supply chain. It could not tell them where.

That three-month gap was not negligence. It was the direct consequence of building a compliance programme on a supplier relationship layer that stops at Tier 2.

The gap between notification and action is exactly the gap that material-level tracing closes.
